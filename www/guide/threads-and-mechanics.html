Threads, gears and other parts

Besides the basic shapes, the library contains some parts that would be tedious to put together yourself. All of them can be changed afterwards in their settings.

Threads

Choose “Thread” in the shape library. Under “Type” you decide what it should be:

  • “Threaded rod”: a cylinder with thread, for example a screw without a head.
  • “Screw”: with a head. You choose the “Head”: “Socket head”, “Countersunk” or “Hex head”.
  • “Nut”: the matching nut.
  • “Tapped hole”: a tapped hole. It is a hole. Drag it into a part, group both, and the part has an internal thread.

You pick the size under “Size” from a list: metric from M2 to M24, the inch sizes UNC and UNF from No. 4 to 1 inch, and the pipe threads G1/16 to G4. If you choose “Custom”, you set “Diameter” and “Pitch” yourself. For an inch size or a G size the field asks for “Threads per inch” instead of the pitch in millimetres.

The list groups the sizes by series, and a line under the field names the series of the size you picked, with examples you know from everyday life:

  • M, metric coarse: the ISO metric coarse thread to ISO 261, as on standard screws and nuts.
  • UNC, US inch coarse: the camera and tripod screw is 1/4"-20 UNC (ISO 1222), larger tripod heads use 3/8"-16 UNC.
  • UNF, US inch fine: the same diameters as UNC with a finer pitch (ASME B1.1).
  • G, pipe thread: a shower hose connects with G1/2, a washing-machine inlet hose with G3/4. A garden tap ends in G3/4, smaller taps in G1/2; that is where the tap connector of a click-on hose system screws on.

The G sizes are the parallel Whitworth pipe threads to ISO 228-1, found on fittings for water, gas, hydraulics and pneumatics. The size names the pipe, not the thread: a G1 measures 33.249 mm across the thread. Choosing a G size sets the profile to “Whitworth (55°)”, and choosing a metric or inch size sets it back to “V-thread (ISO)”. A “Trapezoidal” or “Round” profile you picked yourself stays. The standard only defines the thread, so the head of a screw and a nut on a G size get the same proportions as on a diameter you set yourself. The tapered pipe threads R, Rc and Rp are not included.

More settings:

  • “Direction”: “Right-hand” or “Left-hand”.
  • “Profile”: “V-thread (ISO)” is the standard profile of metric and inch threads, “Whitworth (55°)” that of the G pipe threads: 55° with rounded crests and roots. “Trapezoidal” and “Round” have flat crests and roots. That usually prints more reliably because no thin tips result.
  • “Custom (trapezoid)”: A trapezoid you shape yourself, with a flat crest and a flat root of the same width. “Flank angle” is the angle between the two flanks of a tooth: 60° is the sharp V thread, 30° the trapezoid thread, and at 90° or less no flank is flatter than 45°, so the thread prints without supports. “Thread depth” and “Core diameter” are one measure said two ways: the core diameter is the diameter minus twice the depth, and changing one changes the other. A new own profile starts with the V thread's depth at 60°. The depth has limits that depend on the angle and the pitch: not shallower than a tenth of the pitch, not deeper than 40 % of the diameter, and not so deep that the two flanks take more than nine tenths of the pitch. If you change the pitch, the depth follows in proportion; if you scale the thread, so does the depth. The play and the chamfers work as with the other profiles. Through the MCP bridge the profile is custom with threadFlankAngle and threadDepth.
  • “Clearance (XY)”: For nut and tapped hole, the room that makes sure a printed pair really turns. The coarser your printer works, the more clearance the pair needs. It is the extra room across the whole diameter, and the gap between the flanks of a V thread is only about half of it, so a printed pair needs more than you might expect: new nuts start at 0.4 mm, 0.3 is tight and 0.5 loose. Print a test pair first. Rods and screws have a clearance of their own here, which makes the bolt that much thinner in diameter. It is set to 0. Which side gets the room depends on what you pair:
  • Printed screw and printed nut: leave the screw at 0 and give the nut the room (the 0.4 mm it starts with).
  • Real screw in a printed nut or tapped hole: the printed side takes all of it, so the nut keeps its 0.4 mm. A tighter 0.3 mm is worth a test if the screw wobbles.
  • Printed screw in a real nut: the real nut has no play, so the screw has to be thinner: 0.3 to 0.4 mm here, as much as a printed nut would have.
  • “Play along the axis (Z)”: For nut and tapped hole, the play along the axis, on top of the “Clearance (XY)”. Every tooth of the nut gets this much thinner, the diameter stays as it is. The reason: the “Clearance (XY)” works on the diameter only and opens the flanks of a V thread to about 0.58 of its value. Anyone who wants air between the flanks has to open the diameter wide and gets sideways slop for it. The play along the axis sets the two apart. It is set to 0, so a saved thread does not change, and it goes up to a fifth of the pitch at most, because above that nothing would be left of the nut's teeth. The field exists for nut and tapped hole only; rod and screw keep their own clearance. More below under "Threads for 3D printing".
  • “Chamfer, top end”, “Chamfer, bottom end”, “Head chamfer” and “Rim chamfer”: Chamfers at the ends so the thread starts cleanly and the head has no sharp edge. Each end of a rod, nut or tapped hole has its own chamfer, so the top can be long and the bottom short or none. A screw has only one at its tip, labelled “Chamfer”. On a nut or tapped hole the chamfer is the room the mouth opens beyond the outer diameter; the cone runs at 45 degrees on down to the thread core, so no ledge is left at the outer diameter.

Switching a thread between “Threaded rod” and “Tapped hole” keeps its length. A nut and a screw start from their standard measures.

  • “Quality”: How finely the thread is calculated. Higher is more exact, but slower.

Printed threads are a topic of their own. Make the screw-and-nut pair as a test first before you print a large part, and adjust the clearance to your printer.

Threads for 3D printing

A printed thread needs air in two places, and they act differently:

  • Clearance in the diameter (“Clearance (XY)”): makes the nut wider. The screw then has half the value as air on each side, 0.2 mm at 0.4 mm. The thread turns easily, but the screw can wobble sideways.
  • Play along the axis (“Play along the axis (Z)”): makes the teeth of the nut thinner. The screw can then move back and forth along the axis by that value when it is not tightened. There is no extra air to the side for it.

Together they make the gap between the flanks: about 0.58 times the clearance in the diameter plus the play along the axis. An example for a 0.3 mm gap: it used to take 0.5 mm in the diameter, that is 0.25 mm of air on each side. Now 0.2 mm in the diameter (0.1 mm on each side) and 0.2 mm along the axis do it, and the gap is 0.32 mm.

Consequences to know

  • The screw has play along the axis. Without load it can move back and forth by that value. For fastenings, lids and housings that does not matter; for a lead screw that has to position exactly it does, so keep it small or at 0 there.
  • The nut's teeth get thinner. With 0.3 mm at a pitch of 1.75 mm every tooth is almost a third thinner at its middle and so clearly less strong. For heavily loaded parts take a coarser thread instead.
  • More to calculate. The tooth shape gets extra points, so the thread has more triangles and takes a little longer to compute.
  • No true arc in the STEP export for the Whitworth profile. A “Whitworth (55°)” thread with play along the axis goes to the edge tool and the STEP export as a tooth shape made of straight pieces, not with the true circular arcs. At 0 everything stays as before.

Tips

  1. A test pair first. A screw and a nut, each only 4 to 6 mm tall, take a few minutes. Print three pairs with different play side by side and take the one that turns easily and does not wobble.
  2. Start with values that often fit. Clearance in the diameter 0.2 to 0.3 mm and play along the axis 0.2 mm. If the screw wobbles sideways, lower the clearance in the diameter; if it jams, raise the play along the axis. If you would rather do without the play along the axis, use 0.4 mm in the diameter as before.
  3. The play along the axis should be at least one layer height. What lies below one layer the printer does not resolve. With a layer height of 0.2 mm, 0.2 mm is a good start; with 0.1 mm, less is enough.
  4. Print screw and nut upright. The axis points up, so every turn lies in whole layers and the flanks come out clean. Lying down, the flanks sag.
  5. Fine pitches are hard. Below about 1 mm of pitch the turns often fuse. A coarser thread, or a “Trapezoidal” profile with flat crests, prints more reliably than a fine V thread.
  6. Avoid thin tips. The V thread has a narrow tip that the printer hardly hits with its nozzle width. The “Trapezoidal” and “Round” profiles have flatter teeth.
  7. Deburr and run it in. The first turns are often stiff because small remains of the print stand on the flanks. Turn it back and forth a few times and it runs easier. The chamfer at the start helps threading it in.
  8. Another printer or filament. The play depends on the printer, the material and the nozzle. Print the test pair again when you change either.

The edge tool takes a thread as its exact body. You can chamfer or round the part that carries it with the thread already in place, or the rims of a screw head, see Breaking edges and hollowing bodies. Working out a thread's edges takes a while: a few seconds for an M6, about half a minute for a G1/2 pipe thread. The quickest way to break the edge of a screw head is still the head chamfer in the properties.

Gears

“Gear” comes as “Spur gear”, “Helical gear”, “Herringbone gear” and “Bevel gear”, and as “Ring gear” and “Rack” (further down). New gears have “Involute” under “Tooth Shape”: teeth with involute flanks, as gear generators and engineering use them. Such teeth roll cleanly on each other. You set the number of “Teeth” (6 to 500) and the “Module (mm)”, and the size follows: the outside diameter is module × (teeth + 2), the pitch circle module × teeth. New gears start at module 2. Two gears mesh when they have the same module and the same “Pressure Angle” (usually 20°) and their centres stand (teeth + teeth) × module / 2 apart. Select both gears and the properties panel gives that centre distance and how far apart the centres are now. If the gears should stay where they are, it also names the module at which they would mesh there, and “Fit module of both” gives both gears that module in one step; each keeps its teeth, height and centre hole. If a tooth then meets a tooth instead of a gap, turn one of the gears by half a tooth, which is 180° divided by its number of teeth. “Backlash” takes a little off the teeth so a printed pair does not bind. The value is for the pair, and each gear takes half of it; 0.2 mm is a good start, 0 gives the exact gear. Below the base circle the flank runs straight down to the root and is not undercut as a cutter would do it. That suits printing, but a very small pinion below about 17 teeth may rub slightly at the root.

A helical gear adds the “Helix Angle”. With involute teeth it is the true helix angle on the pitch circle: two helical gears mesh when they have the same module and the same angle, one positive and the other negative. The “Herringbone gear” is two helical halves turning opposite ways: the teeth run one way up to half the height and back after it, like an arrow. That has two advantages for 3D printing: the gear centres itself on its shaft, and it has no axial thrust, because the forces of the two halves cancel. A helical gear, in contrast, pushes sideways along its axis. You set the “Helix Angle” and the “Helix quality” as for the helical gear; the angle applies to each half. Two herringbone gears mesh when they have the same module and the same angle with opposite signs, one positive and the other negative. There is no groove in the middle, as on milled herringbone gears: the two halves run into each other without a step. The edge tool and the STEP export take the herringbone gear as a triangle mesh, not as an exact body.

The bevel gear takes the involute teeth and makes them smaller towards its top. That is an approximation, not an exactly worked out bevel pair.

“Round” are round teeth made of arcs, like Tinkercad's Useful gear: tip and gap run into each other without a corner. Small gears print more forgivingly that way and turn more easily, and on a knob the teeth make a pleasant grip. Round teeth are set by “Teeth”, “Module (mm)” and “Backlash” too; they have no pressure angle. They stand lower than involute teeth, so the outside diameter is module × (teeth + 1.2). Round gears with the same module mesh, at the same centre distance as involute ones; with an involute gear they do not fit.

“Simple” are the straight teeth every gear had so far. There you set “Tooth Size” and “Tooth Width” by hand. Older designs keep them until you change the tooth shape. All three tooth shapes have the “Center Hole”.

The “Ring gear” has its teeth pointing in: a ring that a smaller gear runs inside, like the outer wheel of a planetary set. You set it like an involute or round gear by “Teeth”, “Module (mm)” and “Backlash”, plus the “Rim”, the ring outside the teeth. Its outside diameter is module × (teeth + 2.5) plus twice the rim, with round teeth module × (teeth + 1.7). A gear of the same module runs in it when its centre stands (ring teeth − its teeth) × module / 2 from the ring's centre; select both and the properties panel gives that distance. For a planetary set, ring teeth = sun teeth + 2 × planet teeth, for example 30 = 12 + 2 × 9. A ring gear has no centre hole, it is the ring itself. Between 26 and 33 teeth its tips are up to a quarter module shorter, because a tiny edge would otherwise form at the base circle; meshing is not affected.

The “Rack” is the gear of endless radius: a bar with teeth along one side, whose flanks are straight lines at the pressure angle with involute teeth. Its width (along X) is teeth × pitch, the pitch being π × module, so racks of one module line up end to end, each end falling in a gap. Its length (along Y) is the thickness of the bar and free, as long as the teeth and half a millimetre of bar fit in. The teeth point to the front, towards the viewer of the home view; turn the rack as you need it. A gear of the same module rolls on it when its centre stands its teeth × module / 2 in front of the pitch line; the pitch line lies a module behind the tooth tips, 0.6 module with round teeth. Ring gear and rack come with involute and with round teeth, not with simple ones; switching the gear type keeps the module.

The edge tool and the STEP export take every gear as its exact body, involute flanks as smooth curves. A helical gear on an oval footprint stays a triangle mesh, and so does a helical gear with involute teeth from about 31 teeth. Small teeth limit how far edges can be rounded: on a gear with simple teeth, 30 mm across with 12 teeth the flat between two teeth at the foot is only about 0.65 mm wide, so rounding every edge works up to about 0.5 mm, and on a bevel gear, whose top is smaller, up to about 0.4 mm. A helical gear's teeth lean, which narrows that flat across its slanted edges: the larger the helix angle against the gear's height, the smaller the rounding that fits. The edges of its two flat ends can be rounded, but chamfering just those edges fails in the CAD kernel; round them, or chamfer all edges together at a smaller size, such as 0.2 mm.

Springs

The “Spring” has “Turns” and a “Wire”. Together with the height they decide how soft the spring is. “Winding” sets which way the wire runs: “Right-hand” like a usual compression spring, or “Left-hand”.

The edge tool and the STEP export take a spring as its exact body: a round wire along a helix, cut square at both ends. You can round or chamfer those two wire ends, for instance.

Bent tubes

A “Bent Tube” consists of up to twelve sections: a straight piece followed by a bend. For each you set the “Straight length”, the “Bend angle”, the “Bend radius” and the “Roll angle”. The section you are editing is lit up in orange on the tube, so you can follow the order. A roll angle of 0° bends within the plane of the workplane, at 90° the tube bends upward. The profile can be round, square, hexagonal or octagonal, the inside likewise, or fully solid. If the tube runs into itself, PrintCaddy warns you.

Loft

The “Loft” joins one outline at the bottom to another at the top, like a loft in Fusion: a hose adapter from one diameter to another, a square fan onto a round duct, a stand that turns round towards the top. For each end you pick “Bottom Outline” and “Top Outline”: “Circle / ellipse”, “Rectangle” with “Bottom Corner Radius”, or “Polygon” with its number of corners, plus the width and length of each end and the height. “Top Offset in Width” and “Top Offset in Length” move the top end sideways for a slanted transition; the bottom end stays where it is.

“Wall Thickness (0 = solid)” turns the solid body into a tube open at the top and the bottom. The opening is smaller by the wall at both ends, measured across; where the wall slants it is a little thinner. Drag the frame bigger and both ends grow along, while the corner radius and the wall keep their size.

“Smooth transition (%)” makes the transition smooth. At 0 the sides run straight from the bottom to the top, as on a cone. At 100 they leave both ends straight up and change their size in between along an S-curve: a hose adapter that flares smoothly from a narrow to a wide pipe, instead of running like a funnel. In between the slider mixes the two. A smooth transition works together with “Wall Thickness (0 = solid)”, a twist, a tilt and a sideways offset; a tube with a wall stays open at both ends. It is an exact body like the straight one, so its edges can be chamfered and filleted, but it is built through several sections and takes a little longer to calculate.

“Segments per circle” sets how finely the circles and rounded corners are drawn: how many straight pieces a full circle is made of in the view and in STL, OBJ and 3MF. It starts at 96, which is smooth for most parts; for a large round end you want to see without facets, raise it, up to 360. More pieces mean larger files. A polygon keeps the “Bottom Corners” you set. The field shows when an end is round or has a rounded corner.

“Twist” turns the section evenly about the vertical on the way up, up to 360° either way; the sides then wind round. “Top Tilt, Front/Back” and “Top Tilt, Left/Right” tilt the top end, up to 45°: a positive value raises its front edge or its right side. The sections in between tilt by their share, so the wall runs smoothly into the slant. The highest point stays at the set height. If the height later becomes so small that the slanted end would reach under the plate, the tilt gives way until it fits. A bend along a path, such as a pipe elbow, is not something the loft does.

The loft is an exact body, twisted and tilted too: its edges can be chamfered and filleted, and STEP export keeps it. Add a flange with screw holes as usual and group them.

Gridfinity

The “Gridfinity” shape makes parts for the open storage system by Zack Freedman: bins on a 42 mm grid that stand in a baseplate and stack on one another. The measures follow the published Gridfinity specification, so a bin from PrintCaddy fits a baseplate made with another generator, and the other way round. Under “Type” you choose:

  • “Bin”: a bin with a foot under every grid unit. “Units across” and “Units deep” count grid units of 42 mm (a bin is 0.5 mm smaller, 41.5 mm for one unit); “Units high” counts height units of 7 mm, up to 20. The “Stacking lip” on top of the wall holds the feet of the next bin and makes the bin 4.4 mm taller: a bin of 3 units with the lip is 25.4 mm tall. With “Magnet holes” every foot gets a hole for a 6 × 2 mm magnet in each corner.
  • “Baseplate”: the plate the bins stand in, 5 mm tall, with a socket for every grid unit. It is an open frame with no floor and no magnet holes.
  • “Tray”: a tray of any size with a flat bottom, for board game inserts and parts that need no grid. “Length”, “Width” and “Height” are free in mm, 10 to 252 mm across and deep and 3 to 140 mm high. It has no feet, no stacking lip and no magnet holes, so it does not stand in a baseplate or stack, and its corners are rounded by 3.75 mm, as a bin's are. Everything below about a bin's inside works on a tray too: its floor is 1.2 mm thick until you set it, its dividers and label shelves run up to its top, they and its finger scoops come at any height that leaves them room, and it takes as many compartments as leave each at least 6 mm wide, up to 24 along a side and 40 in all. A bin turned into a tray keeps its size, a baseplate turned into one is 20 mm high, and a tray turned back into a bin or a baseplate is rounded to grid units; with “Tray” as the type under “Shape defaults” in “Workspace settings”, a new tray starts 80 mm wide, 40 mm long and 20 mm high.

The size of a bin or baseplate is always whole grid units (or cells of 21 mm on the half-size grid below), up to 6 × 6 (252 mm, which fits a 256 mm print bed), plus a baseplate's padding (see below); a drag on a handle snaps to the nearest unit. The wall (0.95 mm), the floor (1.2 mm) and the depth of the magnet holes (2.4 mm) are not in the specification; the depth of the holes is fixed, and the wall and the floor can be set (see below). The sizes in this section are for the 0.95 mm wall. The specification's M3 screw holes are not made. The top of the lip is sharp, as the specification draws it; if your printer leaves it ragged, chamfer it with “Lip chamfer”, up to 1 mm, which is the edge tool's chamfer of that size built in, or round it with the edge tool. A bin stacked on top rests on the lip's slope right up to the top, so a chamfer takes the top of that away: about a tenth at 0.25 mm and over a third at 1 mm. Bin, baseplate and tray are exact bodies, so fillet, chamfer and the STEP export work on them. To divide a bin, group thin boxes into it as walls: stand them on the floor, 5.95 mm up (1.2 mm in a tray, or on the raised floor), and on a bin with the lip keep their top at least 0.5 mm below 7 mm per height unit, measured from the underside of the bin (20.5 mm for 3 units), because the feet of the next bin reach 0.35 mm down into the bin. Print bins and baseplates without supports, standing as they are on the print bed.

A bin can also be made on a half-size grid: switch on “Half-size grid (21 mm)”, and then “Units across” and “Units deep” count cells of 21 mm, up to 12 (the same 251.5 mm as 6 units). Every cell has a foot of its own, 20.5 mm across at the top with the specification's steps, and four of them stand in one socket of an ordinary baseplate, so a bin of 1½ or 2½ units fits beside whole ones. When “Half-size grid (21 mm)” is switched on, the bin keeps its outline (2 units become 4 cells); when it is switched off, an odd number of cells is rounded up to whole units. The lip follows the bin's outline as before. With “Magnet holes” each foot gets one hole, 2.5 mm both ways from its middle towards the corner of its cell that lies on the 42 mm grid, counted from the bin's left back corner: the holes line up with those of a baseplate with magnets from another generator when that corner stands on a corner of the grid; a bin of an odd number of cells can also be turned by 180°, so that this corner stands on a corner of the grid at the front right. The baseplate stays on the 42 mm grid. The half-size grid is not part of the specification.

A bin of 3 height units or more can have label shelves along its inner walls: switch on “Label shelf at the back”, “Label shelf at the front”, “Label shelf on the left” or “Label shelf on the right”, in any combination. Each shelf that is on gets its own “Shelf width”, “Shelf length” and, for a shorter shelf, “Shelf position”; the corner settings below them apply to all of them. A shelf is flat on top for a label, with a 1.2 mm strip down at the front and an underside rising at 36 degrees. Its top is 0.85 mm below 7 mm per height unit, measured from the underside of the bin (20.15 mm for 3 units), so the feet of a bin stacked on the lip stay 0.5 mm clear of it. A bin made lower than 3 units shows no shelves; they come back at 3 units. Label shelves are not part of the specification.

“Shelf width” is how far a shelf reaches into the bin: 14 mm, at most 17.2 mm in a bin of 3 units (less with a raised floor), and 16.8 mm for two shelves facing each other in a bin 1 unit deep with 2 mm corners. With “Shelf length” at 0 a shelf runs from wall to wall. A shorter one, at least 10 mm, brings up “Shelf position”, which moves it from the middle of its wall: to the right on the back and front walls, as the front view shows them, and towards the front on the side walls. An end that comes within 1 mm of a wall (1.8 mm with the stacking lip, whose support leans in there), or that stops on that wall's shelf, runs on into it, and two shelves that meet in a corner join into one. Where a shelf meets a wall or another shelf and there is room, the corner is rounded or chamfered: choose “Round” or “Chamfer” under “Shelf corners”, and the size with “Corner size” (2 mm; 0 leaves it sharp). With “Round”, a shorter shelf's free ends have their front corners rounded too, by the same size but at most half the shelf's width.

A bin can also have a finger scoop along any of its inner walls, so that small parts can be pushed out up the curve instead of being picked out where the floor meets the wall: switch on “Finger scoop at the back”, “Finger scoop at the front”, “Finger scoop on the left” or “Finger scoop on the right”, in any combination. The floor rises along a quarter circle into the wall, from wall to wall, and two scoops on neighbouring walls meet in their corner. A bin with a scoop also has every floor edge rounded by 2.8 mm, the radius of its inner corners, and the scoop is that rounding made larger on its wall. Once a scoop is on, “Scoop radius” appears below the switches and applies to all of them: until you set it, it is half the height from the floor up to where the lip's support leaves the wall, 1.65 mm below 7 mm per height unit, measured from the underside of the bin, or up to the top of a bin without the lip (6.7 mm in a bin of 3 units with the lip, less with a raised floor). It is at least 3.5 mm and stays at least 0.5 mm less than that height, and across the bin it leaves at least 2 mm of floor, between two scoops facing each other or between a scoop and the wall opposite it. A bin of 1 height unit has no room for a scoop and shows no switches for it; they come back at 2 units. Finger scoops are not part of the specification either.

A bin can also have a raised floor, for a bin blank to cut shapes into: “Floor height” sets the floor's height, measured from the underside of the bin (5.95 mm, the 1.2 mm floor on the feet, until you set it), and the bin is solid up to it. With the lip it goes up to where the lip's support leaves the wall, 1.65 mm below 7 mm per height unit (19.35 mm for 3 units), so the lip stays whole and the feet of a bin stacked on it stay 1.3 mm clear; without the lip it goes up to the top, and the bin is a solid block. Group holes in the shapes of your tools into it, as deep as you need. Finger scoops start on the raised floor, and label shelves reach less far into the bin so that their underside still ends above the floor; once the floor leaves them no room, their switches go (the scoops above 15.35 mm, the shelves above 15.54 mm, in a bin of 3 units with the lip) and come back when you lower it. A bin of 1 height unit with the lip and a wall of 1.6 mm or less has no room to raise its floor and shows no field for it. A raised floor is not part of the specification either.

“Wall thickness” sets the wall in from the outside, which never changes, so the bin still fits and stacks as any other. It is 0.95 mm until you set it, from 0.8 mm (two lines of a 0.4 mm nozzle) up to where the walls meet. The inner corners are rounded by 3.75 mm less the wall, and square from a wall of 3.75 mm; the rounded floor edges follow them. With the lip, a wall over 1.75 mm stops at a flat ledge 0.85 mm below 7 mm per height unit, so a stacked bin's feet stay 0.5 mm clear, and the floor goes no higher than that ledge. Where the walls leave less than 1 mm inside, the bin is built solid and “Floor height” is hidden. Finger scoops and label shelves without room are left out, and fewer compartments are built and offered. A bin open in full on all four sides has no wall to set. A bin switched open on all four sides is never built solid; a side whose wall leaves no room for its opening is open in full.

On a bin, “Corner radius” rounds its outer corners less than the specification's 3.75 mm, down to 0 for square ones, so bins side by side in a drawer leave only their 0.5 mm gaps where four of them meet. Only the outer wall takes it, from 0.5 mm over the feet up: the feet, the inside and the slopes of the lip a stacked bin rests on keep their rounding, so the bin fits in a baseplate and stacks as before, and a bin of one unit still sits down on the slopes of its socket. Over a square corner the wall ends flat, level with the top of the lip (the bin's top without one) or with the foot of its chamfer. A corner on an open side is square already.

A bin can also be divided into compartments without grouping walls into it: under “Compartments”, just below “Properties”, “Columns” and “Rows” set how many, up to 4 per grid unit each way (2 per cell of the half-size grid) and 40 in all. The dividers run the full height, from the floor (or the raised floor) up to 0.85 mm below 7 mm per height unit, where the label shelves stop too, so a bin stacked on the lip sits as before. Dividers need a bin of 2 height units or more, with the floor at least 3 mm below their top; with less room the bin is built without them, and they come back once it has room again. “Divider thickness” is 1.2 mm until you set it, from 0.8 to 3 mm. The dividers start evenly spaced. To move one, pick it under “Between columns” or “Between rows”, counted from the left or from the back, and set its “Position”, measured from the inside of the left or back wall to the middle of the divider; every compartment stays at least 6 mm wide. Once a divider has been moved, the dividers running that way keep their places in millimetres when the bin is resized; a new number of compartments spaces that way's dividers evenly again. Each compartment has the bin's inner corners, rounded by 2.8 mm, and its floor edges are rounded by 2.8 mm too, with or without a finger scoop. Finger scoops are repeated in every compartment: with a scoop on the back wall, each compartment has one on its back side, against the wall or a divider. In a narrow compartment the radius is made smaller so that 2 mm of floor stays flat, and a compartment too narrow even for 3.5 mm loses the scoops that run that way; a note under “Scoop radius” says so. Dividers are not part of the specification either.

A bin with dividers can have a label shelf in each compartment instead of the shelves along its walls: those are for bins without compartments, and their switches come back once the bin is one compartment again. In a bin of 3 height units or more, choose a compartment with “Row” and “Column” (each shown when there is more than one), and under “Label shelf” the side its shelf stands against, the bin's wall or a divider; a side is only offered while a shelf there still leaves the compartment open. “Shelf length (%)” runs from 10 to 100; a shelf that would come out shorter than 4 mm is left out, and a note under it says so. A shorter shelf brings up “Shelf position”: “Left”, “Middle” or “Right” on a back or front side, or “Back”, “Middle” or “Front” on a left or right side. “Shelf width” is 14 mm until you set it, less in a narrow compartment, and a shelf never reaches so far that it closes its compartment off: at least 6 mm stays open in front of it. The shelves have the profile of those along the walls, and “Shelf corners” and “Corner size” under “Properties” apply to them as well.

A bin can also be open on any side, so that bins standing side by side make one long run: switch on “Open at the back”, “Open at the front”, “Open on the left” or “Open on the right”, in any combination; with all four only the floor on its feet is left. An open side has no wall and no stacking lip above the floor. The walls beside it and their lip run straight out to the bin's outline and end square, so two open bins next to each other are 0.5 mm apart like any two bins, and the floor runs flat out to the edge and ends square there. A label shelf or finger scoop on an open side is not built and its switch goes, and so do that side's scoops against the dividers; the setting is kept for when you close the side again. Shelves and scoops on the walls beside it, and dividers that meet it, run out to the edge, and a compartment offers no label shelf on an open side; one already chosen there is left out, and a note under “Label shelf” says so. The feet and the magnet holes stay as they are. A bin stacked on an open bin is held by the lip on the closed sides only. A bin without the lip whose floor is raised to the top is a solid block and shows no switches for open sides. Open sides are not part of the specification either.

A side switched open can also be opened only partly, for a slot to reach into or a lower wall at the front. Under the switches, each open side has its own rows, named here for the front: “Depth at the front” is the opening's depth, measured down from the top of the lip (from the bin's top without the lip), at least 2 mm; “Width at the front” its width along the side, at least 5 mm; “Position at the front” its position, “Left”, “Middle” or “Right”; and, at an end, “Distance at the front” its distance from that end. To begin with the opening is the whole side down to the floor, which is the open side described above. A depth within 0.5 mm of the floor goes down to the floor, and a width within 0.5 mm of the whole side is the whole side. Anything less keeps the side's wall, its rounded corners and the lip, and cuts a notch with a flat bottom and square ends out of them: down to the floor a slot, across the whole side a lower wall. The opening stays on the straight part of the wall between the corners, or runs out to the edge beside a side that is open in full, where the wall has no rounded corner; a side whose straight part is shorter than 5 mm is open in full. The floor, the dividers and the lip on either side of the notch stay, so a bin stacked on it still sits on the lip, and the side's label shelf, finger scoop and compartment label shelves stay off as on any open side. On a bin built solid by its wall, a side switched open is open in full. A side opened partly still has a wall, so “Wall thickness” stays even with all four sides switched open. Once a side is opened partly, “Opening corners” (“Round” or “Chamfer”) and “Opening corner size” (0, square, until you set it; up to 10 mm, the row offering as much as the largest corner takes) appear below the open sides' rows and round or chamfer the notches' corners seen from outside the wall, the two at the top and the two at the bottom, alike on every side opened partly. The top corners take the size first and the bottom ones what the depth leaves, so a size over half the depth leaves the bottom corners less, and they stay square once the top ones take all of it. Each corner takes as much of the size as the notch and the wall beside it leave room for, so on an opening placed at an end, the top corner there is no larger than the opening's distance from that end; where a corner would end just beside an edge of the wall, it is made up to 0.5 mm smaller to keep clear of it, and under 0.5 mm it stays square. An opening across the whole side has no top corners, and one run out to the edge beside a side open in full has none at that end. With the lip, a top corner is rounded or chamfered in full on the outer wall only, a little less under a chamfered lip; further in, where the lip slopes down, less.

A baseplate can have padding, for a drawer that is not a whole number of grid units: “Padding at the back”, “Padding at the front”, “Padding on the left” and “Padding on the right” add a solid border at the plate's 5 mm height outside the grid, from 0 to 41 mm on each side. The grid stays whole units of 42 mm and moves away from the padded sides, and the outer corners keep their rounding unless “Corner radius” changes it. The plate's size is the whole plate with its padding; a change of padding keeps the units, and dragging a side changes the units and keeps the padding. To fill a drawer, type its inside size into “Drawer width” and “Drawer depth”: for each, the plate takes as many units as fit, up to 6, and splits what is left, less 0.5 mm so the plate drops in, evenly between the padding on the two sides of that direction; the two rows show the drawer the plate fills. A drawer 230 mm wide gives 5 units, 210 mm, and 19.5 mm of padding, 229.5 mm in all; check that the plate fits your print bed. A drawer that one plate on your bed cannot fill takes several baseplates side by side, each padded only on the sides that face the drawer's walls; set their padding with the padding rows. A bin keeps the padding settings and is built without them; they come back when it is made a baseplate again. Padding is not part of the specification either. Below the padding rows, “Corner radius” sets the radius of the plate's four outer corners, 4 mm as the specification draws them, down to 0 for square corners that sit in a drawer's corners; a drawer whose inside corners are rounded takes a radius at least as large as theirs, up to 4 mm. The sockets keep their rounding, so bins fit as before.

Hinge

The “Hinge” prints in one piece and moves afterwards (print-in-place): two leaves lying open flat on the plate, with alternating knuckles on one axis between them. The pin belongs to the back leaf and runs with play through the knuckles of the front one. You set the “Length (axis)”, the “Width opened” and the “Knuckle diameter”, plus the number of “Knuckles” (odd, so the pin is held at both ends), the “Pin diameter” and the “Leaf thickness”.

“Clearance” is the gap between the moving parts: around the pin, between the knuckles and in front of the leaves. With 0.4 mm a well-tuned printer usually breaks free with a small twist; if the parts fuse, raise it, if it wobbles too much, lower it. Print the hinge lying down as it appears on the plate, without supports. If you would rather use a pin of your own, such as a piece of filament or wire, which is smoother than a printed pin on a small hinge, choose “Without pin (use your own)” under “Style”: the printed pin is left out and the bore, the pin diameter plus the clearance, runs through every knuckle, the ends included. With “Opening angle” you fold the second leaf up about the pin to place the hinge as it stands in your design: 0° lies flat as it prints, 180° is closed. The knuckles stay where they are and the hinge remains one piece; remember to set it back to 0° before printing in place. You can add holes to the leaves or group them into a larger part, such as a lid.

A hinge can also be made “With cones” (under “Style”): every other knuckle then ends in cones that nest into conical recesses of the knuckles beside it. There is no pin and no long bridge that could sag, so it prints lying flat; the cones stand at 45°. It is a mesh, so chamfers and fillets do not work on it and the STEP export holds triangles, and it makes fewer knuckles where they would be too short for a cone. Instead of the number of knuckles you can give the “Knuckle width (max)”: the knuckles are then at most that wide and their number follows from the length; typing a number of knuckles takes the width away again.

“Style” has three choices: “With pin”, “Without pin (use your own)” (the bore for a pin of your own) and “With cones”. With cones, “Cone diameter” sets how big the cones are where they grow from their knuckle; bigger cones hold better, and by default they are as large as fits. The cone hinge has no pin and no bore, so its leaf may be as thick as the whole knuckle: set “Leaf thickness” to the knuckle diameter and the leaves lie flush with the knuckles; such a hinge opens about 90° and no further, because the thick leaves meet.

For screwing the hinge on, set the “Screw holes per leaf”: up to six holes along the axis, at the “Hole diameter” you choose. Under “Holes” they are “Countersunk” by default, at 90° for countersunk screws, on the side that faces the other leaf when the hinge is closed, so the heads lie flush; “Straight” leaves them plain. Without further values the holes share the length evenly and sit in the middle of each leaf; “Hole spacing (along)” and “Hole spacing (across)” set them centre to centre, for instance to match a bought hinge you replace. Every hole keeps half a millimetre of leaf to the edges, the knuckles and the next hole; where fewer fit, there are fewer, and the field says so. “Corner radius” rounds the four outer corners of the leaves. With a pin or without, holes and corners stay exact in the STEP export and for the edge tool.

Under “Leaf shape”, “Fingers” gives each knuckle its own finger, as wide as the knuckle, instead of a leaf on each side; the fingers of the two sides alternate like two combs. As no finger lies under a knuckle of the other side, the hinge folds all the way round, and with the style “With cones” and fingers as thick as the knuckle (“Leaf thickness” up to the “Knuckle diameter”) it closes flat, the fingers of one side between those of the other. No leaf joins the fingers of a side: with the printed pin they hang on it, with cones, or without the pin until your own goes in, they are loose pieces. Join each side to your model, for instance one comb to a box and the other to its lid, or group them with it. “Corner radius” then rounds the tips of the fingers; fingers take no screw holes.

Flexi joints

“Flexi joints” turns a flat body into a figure that moves, such as a fish, a snake or a dragon, printed in one piece (print in place). You draw or build the body yourself, for instance from a sketch or an SVG you extrude. The tool cuts it across its length into links and puts a joint at every cut: one link carries an upright knuckle that is thicker in the middle than at its ends, the next one wraps round it with clearance. So the joint holds without fusing, and every slope is at 45°, so it prints without support. Between the links a wedge stays open, as wide as the links should swing to the side.

Select the body (several parts are joined, holes among them cut away) and click “Flexi joints”. In the window you set along which side it is cut (“Longer side” takes the longer one), how many “Joints” there are, the “Clearance” (0.4 mm suits most printers) and the angle for “Swing to each side”. The joints are sized to the body; without “Joint size to fit the body” you give the “Joint diameter” yourself. Without “Links all the same length” you set how far the first joint lies from the start and the last from the end, so that a head, say, stays one large piece. Where a joint goes, the body should be about 6 mm high and 12 mm wide at least; if one does not fit, the window says which.

The result is a bundle of links (meshes) that you print as it lies. If you do not like the cut, undo it and try other values. A first test print with two or three joints shows whether the clearance suits your printer.

Knurling

The “Knurl” is a round grip with grooves all around, for knobs, thumb wheels and tool handles. Under “Pattern” you pick “Straight”, grooves along the axis as on a control knob, “Crossed”: two slanted rows of grooves cross into small diamonds, or “Round”: grooves and ridges along the axis as a soft wave of arcs, a pleasant grip without sharp ridges. Round grooves stay shallower than half a pitch, or the ridges would bulge at their foot; a depth too great gives way. You set the “Diameter” and the “Height”, the number of “Grooves” and the “Groove depth”; for crossed knurling also the “Groove angle” to the axis. The “Chamfer at the ends” breaks both ends at 45 degrees, as on a turned knob; 0 leaves them sharp.

Straight and round knurling are exact bodies, so their edges can be broken and rounded. Crossed knurling stays a mesh, because as an exact body it would take almost a minute to compute with 30 grooves. For a knob on a shaft, group the knurl with a hole, such as a “Thread” set as a tapped hole. Grooves shallower than 0.4 mm hardly any printer prints cleanly, and PrintCaddy does not set them closer than 0.8 mm around the grip: a thin grip gets fewer of them.

Dovetail

The “Dovetail” is the joint in which two parts lock into each other and can only be slid together sideways. You set the width at the wide end, the “Neck width” and the length of the tail. Copy the tail for the other side and make the copy a hole: “Clearance as a hole” then gives a little play so the joint does not jam after printing.

Tip: A tapped hole cuts into the part it overlaps when grouped. So put it a bit into the material rather than just touching the surface.

Teardrop hole

A hole lying on its side gets a bridge across its top when printed, and the bridge sags. The “Teardrop hole” therefore has a point on top: every layer rests on the one below, and the hole comes out round enough without supports. Set the “Diameter (round part)” to the size of the hole, about 3.4 mm for an M3 screw, and the “Hole length” long enough to reach through the part. The “Tip angle” is 90°, so the flanks meet at 45°. Most printers handle that cleanly; a flatter point needs more height, a steeper one saves it.

Make the teardrop a hole and group it with the part. Its length runs along the depth and the point faces up. For a hole in the other direction, turn it by 90° around the vertical axis.

A tapped hole on its side sags at the top in the same way. Give it a teardrop of its own: add a “Teardrop hole” hole whose “Diameter (round part)” is the core of the thread, the diameter its teeth leave free (about 8.4 mm for M10), the same length as the tapped hole, and select both. Click “Align” and bring them into line in the middle - the middle of the teardrop is the middle of its round part, not of its box with the tip, so the two circles lie on each other - and group both with the part. The thread is cut as usual and the tip opens the top of the hole, so it prints without supports.

Counterbore and countersink

A screw head often should not stand proud. The “Counterbore” cuts a round pocket for a socket-head screw (DIN 912), the “Countersink” a cone for a countersunk screw (DIN 7991). Both stand with the head end up: the “Head diameter” is the diameter of the pocket or the cone, the “Shaft diameter” the bore below it, the “Total length” the whole length. On the counterbore you set the “Head depth”, on the countersink the “Cone angle” (90° suits countersunk screws). The defaults fit an M3 screw.

The “Double Countersink” is a hole chamfered on both faces, for a plate that is to look neat on its front and its back. It has no entry of its own in the shape library: take the “Countersink” and switch on “Both sides” in its properties (bodies that are a double countersink already show the switch on): a cone at each end opens to the “Head diameter”, with the “Shaft diameter” between them. You set the “Cone angle” and the “Total length” as before. If the hole is too short for two full cones, they meet in the middle at that angle and the shaft stays wider than you set: a head of 10 mm, a shaft of 6 mm, 90° and a length of 2 mm make two cones from 10 down to 8 mm. As a hole it is pushed through a part like the others.

Make the shape a hole and group it with the part. Let its top end a little above the surface the head should sit on, so the pocket opens cleanly. For the pocket of a hex nut, use a “Polygon” with six sides as a hole.
